## Selecting and operating filtration

Choose a portable cleaner with a clean-air delivery rate (CADR) appropriate to
the room; a larger room or higher ceiling needs more clean-air delivery. EPA's
example sizing table for an eight-foot ceiling is 65 cfm for 100 square feet,
130 cfm for 200, 195 cfm for 300, and 390 cfm for 600. Treat these as sizing
estimates, not a guarantee for a Jakarta home. Run the unit at a fan speed
that occupants can tolerate, place it near the breathing zone of the people
most at risk, and replace a loaded filter. For a central HVAC system, EPA says
to use at least MERV 13 or the highest rating the fan can safely handle; a
technician may be needed to check pressure and fit.

CADR is a particle-cleaning rate, not proof that a device removes gases. A
particle filter/HEPA unit will not remove all pollutants; activated carbon can
help with some gases only when there is enough sorbent material, and there is
no widely used gas-performance rating. Avoid ozone-generating devices and do
not make claims that an ionizer, plant, fragrance, or a cheap “HEPA-like” label
will protect against every pollutant.

## Clean-air-room tradeoffs

During a particle episode, close windows and doors when outdoor air is dirtier
than indoor air and use filtration. When outdoor air is cleaner, ventilate to
remove indoor-generated particles and carbon dioxide. Always control indoor
sources: do not smoke or burn incense indoors, use a range hood vented outside
when safe, and avoid frying or other high-particle activities in the clean
room. A cleaner does not compensate for a combustion source in the room.

EPA states that no cleaner removes all pollutants and that effectiveness
depends on room size, filter efficiency, airflow, run time, infiltration,
maintenance, and indoor sources. [clean-air-room]
