## Actions with a plausible direct emissions pathway

Use public transport, shared trips, walking, or cycling where current air
quality, heat, safety, and mobility needs make that reasonable. Avoid needless
idling, maintain the engine and tyres, repair visible smoke, and complete the
required emissions test; a passing test is a compliance action, not proof that
one vehicle explains a citywide episode. Do not burn household waste or leaves
in the open. Reduce indoor combustion and choose cleaner household fuels and
efficient equipment where feasible. These choices can reduce a person's or
household's emissions, but their citywide effect depends on adoption and
system design.

## Actions that influence structural change

Report suspected open burning, smoky vehicles, or industrial violations through
the responsible DKI/KLHK reporting channel and keep the date, location, photo
or video, wind/odour description, and the relevant station reading. Join a
community-monitoring project only if sensors have documented calibration and
do not present a low-cost sensor as an official regulatory measurement. Ask
officials for inspection coverage, test-pass/fail and sanction counts, CEMS
data, source inventories, budgets, and implementation milestones. Participate
in consultations and local planning, and compare promises with dated public
reports.

WHO's hierarchy is important: personal interventions can reduce short-term
exposure, but public policies that reduce emissions are more effective,
sustainable, and equitable. Protecting yourself on a bad day and reducing your
own emissions are different goals; neither makes an individual responsible for
structural enforcement gaps. [individual-emission-actions]
